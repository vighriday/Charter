The forms are the easy part.

You can register a company yourself, online, for very little. What nobody helps with is the contract between the owners: who owns what, who gets to decide what, and what happens if one of them wants out. That is the piece people wish they had written down, three years later.

Here the two owners hold half each, and they said there is no agreed way for one of them to leave. Charter did not make one up. It wrote down that there is no way out, said why, and added the section that settles things when the two of them cannot agree.

It cannot sign for you, even if you ask.

Charter stamps documents it made itself, and it signs its own diary of what it did. It never signs for a person. This is not a setting anybody can switch off. The part of the code that hands a document to a person is walled off from everything Charter can reach, and a test checks that wall on every single build.

The wall went up before there was anything behind it. The first time a file was put in that folder the build broke, exactly as intended, and stayed broken until somebody wrote down in the open which one file is allowed through.

    Where these came from

    Charter did not make these up as it went. There is one fixed list of sections, in one fixed order, written down in the code before any run starts. Most of them are in every contract Charter writes. A few are not, and those are the ones that record something the owners actually decided.

    A note on the word. The document itself calls them articles and numbers them that way, which is how contracts are normally written, so each one below is labelled the way it is labelled in the file. This page says sections, because that is the plainer word. They are the same thing.

    Two of the sections are a matched pair, and exactly one of them is always used. If the owners have an agreed way for somebody to leave and be bought out, the contract gets the section that describes it. If they have not, it gets the section that says so plainly instead. There is no third option where the question goes unanswered. One more section, the one about deadlock, is added only when the shares make an exact tie possible, because a deadlock rule in a company that cannot deadlock is just noise.

    Sections refer to each other by number. If a section that is left out is named by one that is kept, the whole thing stops rather than printing a contract that points at something not in it. That failure is the point: a document that reads perfectly and refers to a clause nobody agreed to is worse than no document.

    The sections

    Neither owner can outvote the other, and Texas law says neither of them can simply walk away. Charter did not choose that. It wrote down what the two of them said, and put the consequence in the contract instead of letting them find it in year three.

    Nothing here has been signed, and the software that wrote it cannot sign it. This is not legal advice, and nothing on this page is a view on whether this contract is right for these two people.

        The one thing it will never do.

        United States law does let software make a deal on your behalf, and you are bound by it. What the law does not do is call that a signature. A signature is something a person does, on purpose, meaning to sign. Software has no intent to lend you. So Charter can get everything ready, and the last act stays yours.

        Not built means there is no code for it at all. It is on the list anyway, because a list you can only trust when something is missing from it is not a list.
