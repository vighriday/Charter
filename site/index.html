The hard part was never the paperwork.

Forming a business on your own is already solved and it is free. What is not solved is writing down an agreement between two people who trust each other completely today and might not in three years.

In this run the two owners hold half each, and said there is no agreed way for one of them to leave. Charter did not invent one. It wrote down that there is no way out, wrote down why, and included the article that decides what happens when the two of them cannot agree.

A limit nobody can check is not a limit.

The software seals documents it made and signs its own record. It never signs for a person. That is not a setting: there is no chain of imports from anything the model can trigger to the module that carries a document to somebody to sign, and a test walks every import on every build and fails if that ever changes.

It was armed on 28 August while that module did not exist. On 30 August the first file was written into that folder and the build failed, exactly as designed, and it stayed failing until somebody wrote down in the open which single file is allowed to reach it.

        The one thing it will never do.

        The law here is more subtle than the slogan. The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, section 14, lets a contract be formed by electronic agents even when no person is aware of it, and the result is attributed to the person to be bound. So the honest claim is not that a machine may not act. It is that the same law defines a signature as a process executed or adopted by a person, with the intent to sign. Forming can be handed over. The signature is the act of a person, and a machine has no intent to lend it.
